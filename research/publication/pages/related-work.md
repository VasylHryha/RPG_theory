## Other ways to understand organization

A pattern can hold together, become part of something larger, or change the conditions around it. Scientists have studied these processes from several directions. RRG starts on shared ground, and readers are right to ask what it adds.

**RRG's hypothesis** joins formation, spreading and change of the [background](/glossary/#background)—the surrounding material, activity and conditions—into one cycle: background → persistent organization → changed background. Its proposed rule, [resonance-fit](/glossary/#resonance-fit), describes which arrangements fit together and last: forces gather and hold, while compatible shape and activity help explain what lasts within and between organizations. This addition has no evidence yet that distinguishes it from the theories below. Spreading also needs suitable conditions; it is not automatic.

## Prigogine: order sustained by flow

An open system far from equilibrium can maintain organized patterns while energy and matter flow through it. Such organization has a thermodynamic cost. RRG shares the picture of activity sustaining a structure. It asks whether structures sustained by an energy flow and ones that remain bound without that flow belong to the same formation–spreading–background cycle, without replacing thermodynamics.

Prigogine, I. [“Time, Structure, and Fluctuations.”](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.201.4358.777) *Science* 201(4358):777–785 (1978).

## Haken: parts and whole shape each other

Synergetics describes interacting parts producing a collective pattern that then constrains their behaviour. RRG shares this feedback. Its proposed extension follows a persistent organization into possible spreading and changed conditions for a next level; that is not yet a distinct rule for which arrangements last.

The existing reference, [“Synergetics’ two foundations and the city”](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjs/s11734-025-01794-7), is Juval Portugali's discussion of synergetics, not a paper by Haken. The source citation gives 2025; the recorded publisher metadata gives 2026.

## Simon: stable steps toward larger forms

Simon explains how stable intermediate forms can become parts of more complex organization. RRG shares that hierarchy. It additionally asks how shape and activity sustain each intermediate, how it may spread, and how it changes what can form next. A hierarchy alone does not establish that explanation.

Simon, H. A. “The Architecture of Complexity.” *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106(6):467–482 (1962). [JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/985254).

## Kauffman and autocatalytic sets: chemical mutual support

In an autocatalytic network, products collectively help catalyze the reactions that sustain the network. RRG shares the idea of organization helping maintain itself. It proposes a broader compatibility rule beyond chemistry; mutual help within a chemical network does not itself establish resonance-fit across physical scales.

Existing readings: Hordijk, Hein & Steel, [“Autocatalytic Sets and the Origin of Life”](https://doi.org/10.3390/e12071733), *Entropy* 12, 1733–1742 (2010); and the Kauffman-related review [“Autocatalytic Networks at the Basis of Life’s Origin and Organization”](https://doi.org/10.3390/life8040062), *Life* 8, 62 (2018).

## Major transitions: a new biological unit

Previously separate entities can become parts of a new unit, changing how reproduction and information transmission work. RRG shares the smaller-to-larger pattern. It asks whether a comparable abstract cycle reaches nonliving levels too; biological heredity and selection are not assumed everywhere.

Szathmáry, E. & Maynard Smith, J. [“The major evolutionary transitions.”](https://doi.org/10.1038/374227a0) *Nature* 374:227–232 (1995).

## Niche construction: life changes its conditions

Organisms modify their environment, which can alter selection for themselves and others. This closely resembles RRG's background feedback. RRG proposes comparable feedback outside life, but must specify mechanisms and measurements rather than simply rename environmental change.

Laland, K., Matthews, B. & Feldman, M. W. [“An introduction to niche construction theory.”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10682-016-9821-z) *Evolutionary Ecology* 30:191–202 (2016).

## Assembly theory: construction history matters

Assembly theory considers the steps needed to build objects and their abundance when examining selection and evolution. It is actively debated. RRG shares attention to earlier construction enabling later possibilities, but focuses on shape–activity support, spreading and background change rather than measuring assembly history.

Sharma, A. et al. [“Assembly theory explains and quantifies selection and evolution.”](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06600-9) *Nature* 622:321–328 (2023).
